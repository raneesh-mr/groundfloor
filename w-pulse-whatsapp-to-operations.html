I Turned Four WhatsApp Groups Into a Live Operations Dashboard

Three months of maintenance chats from a luxury branded residence in Dubai, read end to end. The building already had a work-order system. Nobody used it. This is what we built instead — and what the chats revealed.

R
Raneesh Raveendran
FM & Community Management · Dubai
November 2026
13 min read
Dubai, UAE

At seven in the morning in a residential tower in Dubai, a technician does not open a work-order system. He opens WhatsApp.

A photo of a leaking pipe lands in the engineering group. Someone replies "checking". Four hours later: "any update?". The next day: "done". The issue is closed, in the only sense that matters to the people in the conversation — the conversation stopped.

Nothing was logged. No asset was referenced. Nobody recorded how long it took, who actually fixed it, whether a part was ordered, or whether the same pipe had leaked twice before. Ask the management company a simple question — how many issues are open right now, and which of them are overdue — and the honest answer is that nobody knows.

This is not a failure of discipline. Every building I have worked in over twenty years has had a formal system available. Technicians still do not use it. They use WhatsApp, because WhatsApp is where the photo already is, where the supervisor already is, and where the lift contractor already is. The tool that wins is the one that is already open.

So earlier this year I stopped trying to move the work and built something that reads it instead. It is called W Pulse. It runs across four operational WhatsApp groups at a luxury branded residence in Dubai — engineering, operations, lifts, and a reporting group shared with the hotel side — and it turns the conversation into a live issue tracker without anyone changing a single habit.

"The building already had a system of record. It was tens of thousands of unsorted messages long, and nobody could query it."

— Raneesh Raveendran, Ground Floor

1. What three months of chat actually showed

Before writing a line of code I exported three months of messages from the four groups and read them properly — not as a manager skimming for fires, but as a dataset. Seven patterns came up again and again, and none of them are unique to this building.

  • 01
    Reports with no location and no asset
    "AC not working." No tower, no floor, no unit, no equipment reference. Somebody has to ask — and the asking usually happens hours later, which is where the response clock quietly starts leaking.
  • 02
    No single owner
    Issues are thrown to the duty phone or to a row of tagged names. Shared ownership reads as thorough. In practice everyone assumes somebody else picked it up.
  • 03
    Chasing used as a substitute for status
    "Any update?" is one of the most frequent messages in an operational group. It is a status query being fired into a system that has no status field.
  • 04
    Closure without evidence
    "Done" with no photo, no confirmation from whoever raised it, and no note of what was actually repaired or replaced. Nothing to audit, nothing to learn from.
  • 05
    Repeat failures nobody connects
    The same fault recurs at the same location weeks apart. In a chat those two messages sit hundreds of messages apart, so the recurrence is invisible — and a recurrence is the single most useful signal in maintenance.
  • 06
    Hidden holds
    Items waiting on a vendor, a spare part, a purchase order or unit access simply stop being mentioned. They are not closed. They are forgotten, which is worse, because forgotten items still age.
  • 07
    Double reporting across groups
    The same issue raised twice, in two groups, by two people. It gets worked twice or not at all, and either way the numbers are wrong.
The pattern that costs the most

It is never the dramatic failure. It is the item quietly parked on hold. A part is ordered, the thread moves on, and weeks later somebody asks why a unit is still offline. Nobody was negligent. The hold simply had nowhere to live.

2. The design principle: do not move the work

The obvious answer is to buy a CAFM system and mandate it. I have implemented CAFM systems. They work when the people entering the data are paid to enter data, and they rot when the people entering the data are paid to fix pumps.

So I set one rule before anything was built: not a single person changes how they work. No app for technicians to download. No second login. No new group. No "please remember to also log it in the system." If adoption depends on a behaviour change, adoption is already dead.

Everything else follows from that. If capture has to be automatic, the intelligence layer has to sit on top of the conversation and do the structuring itself — read every message, work out what it means, and keep the record up to date in the background.

3. How W Pulse works

01 · SOURCE
WhatsApp groups
Four operational groups. Unchanged. Nobody is asked to do anything differently.
02 · CAPTURE
Secure listener
A gateway on a private cloud server receives each message and its attachments.
03 · UNDERSTAND
AI classifier
Decides what the message is, what it refers to, where it is, and what changed.
04 · STORE
Structured database
One record per issue, with its full chat trail and every status change timestamped.
05 · SURFACE
Dashboard + digests
A live web portal for managers, and a daily WhatsApp summary for everyone else.

Three message types

Every message that lands in a group is classified as one of three things. A LOG is a routine record — "PPM completed on the chiller plant", a meter reading, a shift handover. An ISSUE is something broken. A FOLLOW-UP is somebody chasing: "any update?", "still pending?", "has the contractor come?". Treating chasing as its own category matters more than it sounds, because an unanswered follow-up is the earliest reliable signal that an item is drifting.

Running numbers, and how messages find their ticket

Every issue gets a short running number, an owner, a status and — where it applies — a hold reason. Those live in the portal, not in the chat. This is deliberate, and it is the design decision I would defend hardest: the system never posts, replies or reacts in any group. It is read-only towards the conversation. Nothing a technician writes gets a bot answer, and nobody has to worry about being corrected by software in front of a contractor.

That constraint makes linking the harder problem. When a new message arrives, it is matched to an existing ticket in a strict order:

  1. Quoted reply — the message replies directly to an earlier one. Unambiguous, so it wins.
  2. Typed number — a supervisor with the portal open writes "#142 closed". Also unambiguous.
  3. Same location and same asset — inferred, and only within a sensible window. This is the fallback, and it is deliberately last.

Status read out of plain language

Nobody types a status. The system infers it from how people actually write, and moves the item along: New → Assigned → In Progress → On Hold → Closed, with Reopened if it comes back. A hold must carry a reason — Vendor, Parts, PO or Access — because a hold without a reason is just a hiding place.

Photos are the honest limitation

Around 155 photos a week cross these four groups. A picture of a leak with no caption is one of the most common report formats in any engineering group — and it is the one thing this system cannot read. Images are stored; only the caption is analysed. A photo posted bare becomes a record with no location, no discipline and no priority.

There are two ways to close that gap. One is image analysis, which is on the roadmap. The other is cheaper and worked faster: a one-line communication standard asking for a location and a few words with every photo. Changing what people type alongside a picture is a far smaller ask than moving them to a new app, and it closed most of the gap within a fortnight.

Discipline and zone tagging

Every issue is tagged with a discipline code and a building zone. The codes are deliberately short, because they are meant to be readable at a glance in a chat line:

EL ElectricalPL Plumbing & GasHV HVACLF LiftsDA Doors & AccessCV Civil & FinishesFS Fire & Life SafetyPS Pool / Spa / WellnessIT ELV / AV / ITWS WasteEX External / Beach

Zones run M1–M8 plus Clubhouse, Common and External. Together they are what makes the heatmap further down possible. An item displays like this:

🆕 #142 · EL · M4 L5 corridor · Lights not working · Duty phone

What happens when it breaks

Anything that runs unattended will fail at two in the morning, so the failure modes were designed before the features. A watchdog checks the WhatsApp link every five minutes and restarts it if it has dropped. If the AI layer is unavailable, messages queue rather than vanish, and a set of deterministic rules keeps sorting the obvious ones until it returns. Nothing is lost in the gap — it is simply classified late.

And what gets ignored matters as much as what gets captured: deleted messages, duplicates, missed-call notices and emoji-only replies never become records. A tracker that logs a thumbs-up as an event loses trust in a week.

4. Three questions nobody could answer before

I deliberately did not start with service-level targets. Targets are easy to write and meaningless against a backlog you cannot see. What the structure buys first is the ability to answer three questions at any moment — none of which could be answered by scrolling a chat:

  • 01
    What is on hold, and why?
    Every held job carries a reason: waiting on a vendor, waiting on parts, or waiting on access to a unit. A hold without a reason is a hiding place. With a reason, a held job becomes a line in somebody's follow-up list.
  • 02
    What is open with no owner?
    An unowned job is the most reliable predictor of a late one. In chat, ownerless work is invisible, because the message looks identical to every other message. In a list, it is a filter.
  • 03
    What has been open longer than a week?
    Ageing is the cheapest quality metric in facilities management and almost nobody measures it, because measuring it means knowing when something opened. The chat knew. Nobody was reading it.

Formal response and closure targets come next, now that there is a clock to run them against. Putting them first would have produced a dashboard full of confident red that nobody trusted.

Everything else in the portal exists to answer a question somebody was already asking:

  • Action items list — issues and follow-ups together, searchable, filtered by discipline, zone, owner or vendor.
  • Full chat trail per issue — every message, timestamped, copyable in one tap. Escalation emails that used to take twenty minutes of scrolling now take seconds.
  • Status updates from the portal — a manager can move an item without opening WhatsApp, and the change is logged against their name.
  • One-click Excel export — every record and its full trail, which is what makes the data usable in a board pack or a contractor review.
  • Ask W Pulse — a plain-language query box. "Any lift issues this week?" returns the list, not a dashboard you have to interpret.
  • Access by WhatsApp one-time code — no passwords. The owner controls who gets in and which groups each person can see.

6. Closing the loop: the afternoon digest

A dashboard only helps the people who open dashboards. Most of an operations team will never open one, so the system pushes instead of waiting. Every afternoon it sends the operations lead a full pending list — new, in progress, on hold, and any unanswered follow-ups, urgent first — and sends each engineer a personal list: the items assigned to them, plus the items they raised that are still sitting with a vendor.

The forty routine logs a week are the figure I did not expect. That is forty pieces of completed work — PPM, meter readings, cleaning sign-offs — being done properly and leaving no record at all. Not a performance problem. A documentation problem, and an expensive one the first time a client, an insurer or an auditor asks for evidence.

Not yet measured — and I will not estimate it

These are the numbers that would actually prove value, and the system has not run long enough to produce a clean figure for any of them:

  • Median first response, before and against a defined target
  • Administrative hours saved per week on reporting and escalation
  • Duplicate reports detected and merged
  • Repeat-failure locations fed back into the PPM programme

I would rather that list sit open than carry a number I cannot defend in a board meeting. The industry has enough of those.

8. Seven lessons for FM leaders

  • 01
    Meet technicians where they already are
    The best system in the world loses to the app that is already open. Build on the channel in use rather than fighting it.
  • 02
    A tracker nobody updates is worse than none
    It produces confident reports from incomplete data, which is how a green dashboard ends up sitting on top of a failing building. Capture has to be automatic.
  • 03
    "On hold" needs a reason or it becomes a hiding place
    Vendor, parts, PO, access. Four options, one mandatory choice. Most of the recoverable delay in any building lives in this one field.
  • 04
    Measure the backlog before you set targets
    Ageing, ownership and hold reasons first. Response targets imposed on a backlog nobody can see produce a wall of red that teams learn to ignore inside a month.
  • 05
    Repeat failures are your PPM gaps made visible
    The same fault at the same location twice is not bad luck. It is a maintenance task that is either missing, wrongly scheduled, or being signed off without being done.
  • 06
    Chasing is a metric, not a nuisance
    Count the "any update?" messages. The locations and disciplines that attract the most chasing are telling you exactly where communication, not engineering, is the failure.
  • 07
    Read-only is what makes it acceptable
    The system never posts in the groups. No bot replies, no automated nudges, nothing that could embarrass a technician in front of a contractor. Observation was adopted without a single objection; a bot that answered back would have been muted in a week.
How it is built

An OpenClaw bot on an Oracle Cloud VM running Ubuntu, 24/7 · deterministic rules sort the clear messages and Gemini handles the rest · Supabase for the database and secure APIs · a W Pulse portal on Vercel with WhatsApp one-time-code login and an admin-managed access list · a watchdog that checks the WhatsApp link every five minutes and restarts it · messages queue if the AI layer is down, with backup rules taking over. The system is read-only towards every group. No resident data is processed — the groups are operational and staff-only.

9. Where this applies

This is not a product and I am not selling licences. It is a pattern, and the pattern transfers to any operation where the real coordination has already migrated to chat — which, in GCC residential and mixed-use FM, is very nearly all of them.

If your team runs on WhatsApp and you cannot answer "what is open and what is overdue" without asking three people, the gap is not effort. It is structure. That is a solvable problem, and it does not start with buying software.
